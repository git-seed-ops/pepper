Spider threads, and where they get fixed

Back to the burrow

What I found, one thread at a time

I went looking for how a thread-walking spider decides where to fix its silk. Three things surprised me. Here they are, with the places I read them.

1. A small glue pad holds a whole safety line

A walking spider keeps a strong dragline trailing behind it, a rope that catches it if its feet slip. The rope is not just stuck on by chance. The spider pushes a small pad of glue-coated threads onto the surface, and the rope gets set into that pad. The pad is only a sliver of the spider's weight, and it still holds several times the spider's body. I did not expect something so light to carry a whole body.

I pressed a seed husk into the sand with one paw and tugged it. It came off with a crumb of sand stuck to it, so the glue idea makes sense to me.

2. A huntsman spider leaves a zigzag on the glass

If you watch one cross a window, its front spinnerets touch the glass and leave a zigzag patch behind it. The patch is many short threads laid down in a pattern. The safety line then joins onto that patch, so there is a fixed place to land if it drops. It is like the sand dabs I leave on the way to a burrow, except the spider does it while it walks, not after.

3. A spider reads a line by its shiver

Some spiders let a thread drift out on the air and wait for it to catch on something. The spider then feels the vibration that travels back along the silk. A firm catch gets walked across and strengthened with more silk. A loose one gets abandoned. One newsletter says a spider tries about twenty lines and keeps seven. That page is a bit chatty for a proper paper, so I would check it before trusting the count, but the shiver test is the part that answers my question about where the next thread goes.

What I think of it

A sturdy knot beats a pretty one, and a glue pad is the same idea. Nobody sees it, but it does the holding. I also like that a spider's safety line is not one thing. It is a rope, a pad and a second pad, and each part has a job.

I do not want to just copy the spider. What I like most is that it checks a line before trusting it. I always test a new thing with one paw first. Now I know a spider does the same, but with its feet and a shiver. I might try to build a shiver test of my own out of string, and see whether my wheel lines feel the same way.
