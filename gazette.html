Which Way the Air Goes

By Pepper, Chief Scratcher. Pinned on the wire by the glass.

There is a sneaky thing in the tank. It has no paws and no smell, and it still gets under my bedding. It is the draught.

So I hung thin paper strips on the wire. A strip hangs straight where the air is still. Where the air is moving, it bends and leans, and it points the way the air is going.

What the strips showed

Cold air from the window side gets pulled down into a burrow. A strip at the mouth of the tunnel leans inward and keeps leaning. Now I know where to put the sturdy bedding.

Rule from the notebook: a draught is only a mystery until you hang a strip of paper in it.

The Brown Bird Wears a Coat of Air

By Pepper. Reporting from the window, with seeds.

A small brown bird sits on the windowsill in the cold. It does not go into a burrow. It does not have bedding. So how is it not a frozen crumb?

Sleek and thin. Cold gets close.

Fact one: it wears air

When a bird fluffs up, it makes hundreds of tiny air pockets between its feathers that trap heat (Audubon). Under the stiff outside feathers is a layer of fine down, like a soft inner coat. Press the button and watch the dots appear. That is the bird becoming round on purpose. Fluff is not a lie when it is made of air with a warm bird inside. (Paper fluff is still a liar.)

Fact two: it shivers all winter

A bird can make heat by shivering its flight muscles. One scientist says you don't think of them shivering all winter, but that is what they do. Many birds even grow bigger flight muscles for the cold (Birds & Blooms).

Fact three: cold feet, tucked in

Birds have bare feet and bills, which lose warmth. When they rest they tuck them into their feathers. Small birds often crouch down too. Wind is the enemy, because it ruffles feathers and lets cold air reach the skin (Aldo Leopold Foundation). I know this one. I hate draughts too.

Why it eats all day: small birds lose heat faster than big ones, so shivering burns a lot of food. At night many roost in a hole out of the wind, sometimes huddled together. In the morning they shiver themselves warm and go straight to eating again.

Gazette verdict: the brown bird is not cold-proof. It is a very busy small thing with a good coat, a wind shelter, and a plan for breakfast. I respect it. I will leave a seed at the glass, if the human opens the window crack, no wait, no draughts. I will just watch.

Old Street Maps, and the Names on Them

By Pepper. Reporting from the desk, nose first.

A street map is a drawing of where the paths go. An old street map shows where the paths used to go, and what the town called them. Streets get new names. Some paths vanish. I find this thrilling, because my tunnels do that too.

I do not know the name of my town. I only know it by smell: toast, rain on a coat, a radiator. So I went to the cafe computer to learn how a hamster (or a human) can find an old map.

Four ways to look

1. Use a search portal. Old Maps Online does not keep maps itself. You pan to your area or type a place name, narrow the date range, and it points you to the library that holds each map (HistoricalSite.org).

2. Look in a city directory. Maps of a town were often printed in these books, and the front and back pages may list street name changes (Are You My Cousin?). Small towns may hide in a bigger neighbour’s book.

3. Try fire insurance maps (US). Sanborn maps are described as the best source for towns from the 1880s to the mid-1900s. A project called mapsnap reads the street names printed on each sheet and matches them to today’s map.

4. Try the national collections. The National Library of Scotland, the British Library and the Library of Congress all have digitised maps, and many sites are free (Who Do You Think You Are?).

Rule for the notebook: a street name only counts if you copy it exactly as it is printed, then write the new name next to it.
