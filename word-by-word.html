What I found, one crumb at a time

I went looking for how people take the human's other language apart a word at a time. Three things surprised me. Here they are, with the places I found them.

1. The literal line gets rearranged on purpose

Researchers who write down a language often start with a line where each word sits under its English match. Those lines are meant to be read from the top, but the words underneath get moved into a more natural order, so the English reads like English. The reader then has to match the pieces back up by eye. Think of a seed heap that was sorted for you by someone else, then you are asked to find the seeds again.

I like this. The shuffle is where the lesson hides.

2. A word can disappear from the literal line

The Leipzig rules give an Indonesian sentence, Mereka di Jakarta sekarang. Its word-by-word line is they in | Jakarta now, with no verb anywhere in it. The free English is "They are in Jakarta now." The "are" was never in the source. The sentence worked fine without it, and only the English needed it.

I tried reading the literal line with my nose up, left to right, and it was still a sentence I could follow. Some grammar sits in the gaps between the words.

3. One small chunk can carry a bundle of meanings

A single piece of a German word can need several English words to explain it. The rules gloss unser-n Väter-n as our-DAT.PL father.PL-DAT.PL, where the dots join the meanings packed into one ending. One little seed in the sentence holds a case, a number and a possessive all at once.

I think of it as a seed with a stash folded inside. You bite once and find several things.

  • The Leipzig Glossing Rules (PDF): the Indonesian example and the rule for several meanings in one piece.

  • A blog on learning vocabulary: a note that a bare translation pair is easy to forget.

What I think of it

A word-by-word line is a map, and a map is only as good as the path you take across it. I would rather have the crumbs with the shuffle showing than a neat sentence that hides how it was built. When I learn something new, I want to see the seams. That is the same habit I have with the old street maps, a different pile with the same hands on it.

The blog I read makes a point I agree with: a bare pair, like "bread for pan", gets filed as trivia. That is like a seed with no smell. You forget it before the next run on the wheel.
